The National Center for Home Food Preservation, run out of the University of Georgia with USDA funding, publishes the research-based guidelines that nearly every state extension office in the US points to for safe canning, freezing, drying, curing, and fermenting food at home. Its recommendations, at nchfp.uga.edu, are the reference point this guide (and most credible home-preservation content) is built on.

This article reframes a search for that organization's name into what actually matters for a home food storage plan: which guidelines exist, what they say about the methods people actually use, and where to go when a specific food or process is not covered here.

## What does a research-based preservation guideline actually mean

A "research-based" recommendation is one tested in a lab for time and temperature needed to destroy the specific pathogens and spoilage organisms relevant to that food and method, not a recipe passed down that happens to have worked so far. This distinction matters because some spoilage organisms, most seriously Clostridium botulinum in low-acid canned foods, can produce a toxin without any visible sign, smell, or taste change in certain foods. A recipe that has "always worked" for a home canner offers no information about whether it reliably reaches a safe internal temperature; a tested process does.

The Center's guidelines specify, for each food and jar size: correct acid level or added acid, processing method (boiling water bath or pressure canner), exact processing time by altitude, and headspace. Deviating from any one of these, even changing jar size without adjusting time, can produce an unsafe product that looks and tastes fine.

## What preservation methods do these guidelines cover

| Method | What it's for | Key variable the guidelines control |
|---|---|---|
| Water bath canning | High-acid foods: fruit, jam, pickles, tomatoes with added acid | Processing time by jar size and altitude |
| Pressure canning | Low-acid foods: vegetables, meat, poultry, seafood | PSI and time by jar size and altitude |
| Freezing | Most fruits, vegetables, meats | Blanching time (vegetables), headspace for expansion |
| Dehydrating | Fruits, vegetables, meats (jerky), herbs | Internal temperature and time to reach safe moisture content |
| Fermenting | Vegetables (sauerkraut, kimchi-style, pickles) | Salt concentration and temperature during fermentation |
| Curing/smoking | Meats | Salt/nitrite ratios, temperature control |

## Why altitude changes processing time

Water boils at a lower temperature as elevation increases, which means the same processing time that is safe at sea level may not reach a high enough temperature at 5,000 feet to destroy spoilage organisms. Every credible set of guidelines, including USDA's and the Center's, publishes an altitude adjustment table; skipping it is one of the most common safety errors in home canning outside of low-acid foods processed in a water bath at all (which is unsafe regardless of altitude).

## How current do these guidelines stay, and why that matters

Recommendations occasionally change as new research narrows safety margins or as equipment changes; an example is the shift away from open-kettle canning and paraffin-sealed jams, both once common and now considered unsafe by current standards. Using an old family cookbook's canning times without cross-checking against current guidance risks following a method superseded decades ago. Checking the processing time and method against current NCHFP or USDA tables before canning a new food, even one you have made before, costs a few minutes and closes that gap.

## Common food-specific answers people look for

- **Tomatoes:** require added acid (bottled lemon juice or citric acid) even though many varieties taste acidic, because acidity varies by variety and ripeness and cannot be judged by taste.
- **Green beans:** must be pressure canned; there is no safe water-bath method for a low-acid vegetable like beans.
- **Salsa:** must follow a tested ratio of low-acid ingredients (peppers, onions) to acid (vinegar or lemon juice); freelancing the recipe changes the acidity and the safe processing time.
- **Jerky:** needs to reach and hold a specific internal temperature, not just look dry, to be shelf-stable at room temperature.

## How these guidelines get updated, and how to keep up

Research-based guidance is revised when new studies narrow a safety margin, when equipment standards change (electric pressure canners, for instance, are still not universally endorsed for canning because their pressure regulation has not been validated the same way stovetop dial and weighted-gauge canners have), or when an outbreak investigation traces back to a specific practice. Most state extension offices post seasonal updates or a canning hotline that runs during peak preserving months, typically July through October in the northern hemisphere. Subscribing to a state extension's food preservation newsletter, most are free, is a low-effort way to catch a guideline change before it affects a canning batch.

## Building a personal reference binder from these sources

A practical approach for a household that cans regularly is to print or save the specific process pages actually used, tomatoes, green beans, salsa, jam, rather than relying on memory or a general impression of "the rules." Processing times differ by jar size within the same food, and it is easy to misremember a quart time as a pint time or vice versa. A one-page reference sheet per food, taped inside a pantry cabinet, removes that risk during a canning session when hands are full and jars are hot.

## Where these guidelines fall short and where to get help beyond them

The published guidelines are conservative and food-specific by design; they do not cover every regional recipe or every combination ingredient home cooks might want to preserve. When a specific recipe is not listed, the safe approach is to can the ingredients separately using tested times for each, or to contact a state extension's master food preserver program, which exists in most states and can evaluate an untested recipe or answer a specific safety question directly, often by phone during canning season.

## A quick self-check before you trust a recipe

Ask three questions before following any canning recipe found outside a cited research-based source: does it name a processing method matched to the food's acidity, does it give a specific time and jar size rather than a vague "process until done," and does it come from or cite a university extension, USDA, or the National Center for Home Food Preservation. A recipe that fails any of the three is worth verifying against a tested source before use, even if it has been shared widely or looks professionally produced.

## FAQ

**Are old canning cookbooks safe to use?**
Not automatically. Cross-check any pre-1990s recipe or one using open-kettle canning, oven canning, or paraffin seals against current guidelines; several older methods are no longer considered safe.

**Do I need a specific book, or is a website enough?**
A current university extension or USDA-affiliated source, updated and specific about jar size, altitude, and processing time, is sufficient. Avoid recipe blogs that do not cite a tested source for their processing times.

**What if my altitude isn't listed exactly?**
Round up to the next listed altitude bracket rather than down; slightly over-processing costs a few minutes of jar quality, under-processing costs safety.

**Can I substitute pressure canning for water bath canning on any recipe?**
Generally yes for high-acid foods if you have a pressure canner and prefer it, using the pressure-canning time and PSI listed for that food. It is the reverse, water-bath processing a low-acid food, that is unsafe.

**Is freezing exempt from these safety concerns?**
Freezing halts microbial growth but does not kill pathogens already present, so normal food safety handling (clean surfaces, proper cooling, safe thawing) still applies; freezing is a much lower-risk method overall than canning because there is no anaerobic sealed environment for botulism to develop in.

Electric multi-cookers marketed with a canning function are a frequent point of confusion; as of the current guidance, no electric pressure multi-cooker has been validated by USDA or NCHFP for safe pressure canning, because none has demonstrated it reliably reaches and holds the required pressure throughout a full processing cycle the way a dedicated stovetop pressure canner does. Households canning low-acid foods should use a dedicated canner with a dial or weighted gauge rather than a multi-cooker's canning setting.

Bottom line: match the food to a tested method and time, adjust for altitude, and treat any recipe without a cited safety source as unverified rather than assuming it is fine because it looks familiar.

A final practical point: guideline documents are written for the general case and occasionally note exceptions for specific regions or crop varieties; when a recipe or process seems to conflict with general guidance for a good reason, such as a state extension publishing a locally adapted variant, that specific, locally validated guidance takes precedence over the general national recommendation for that particular case.
