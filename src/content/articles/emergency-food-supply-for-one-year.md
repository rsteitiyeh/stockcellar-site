A one-year emergency food supply for one adult works out to roughly 730,000 calories, around 450 pounds of food, and 20 to 25 sealed five-gallon buckets. The practical core is white rice, dried beans, rolled oats, pasta, and cooking oil, supplemented with canned protein, powdered milk, and a daily multivitamin to close the micronutrient gaps that dry staples alone cannot cover.

How far you deviate from that baseline depends on household size, dietary restrictions, available storage space, and budget. A single adult building from bulk staples can reach a one-year supply for $450 to $650 in groceries plus $60 to $120 in containers. The same caloric coverage from a prebuilt commercial kit runs $1,200 to $3,500 per person. The gap is significant, and it compounds across a four-person household.

## How many calories does a one-year food supply need to cover?

The USDA Dietary Guidelines for Americans sets 2,000 calories per day as the reference intake for a moderately active adult, which puts the annual target at 730,000 calories per person ([USDA Dietary Guidelines 2020-2025](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/)). Active adults, teenagers, and people doing physical labor in a crisis need 2,500 to 3,000 calories daily, raising the one-year total to 912,500 to 1,095,000 for those individuals.

Children's daily calorie needs vary by age: under 8 requires roughly 1,200 to 1,400 calories; ages 8 to 13 need about 1,600 to 2,000; a teenage boy between 14 and 18 can require 2,800 or more depending on activity level.

Compute each household member's annual calorie requirement separately, add them up, then divide the total by the calorie density of the foods you plan to store. White rice delivers about 1,640 calories per pound; dried beans around 1,530 per pound; rolled oats about 1,700 per pound. The table in the next section shows the full 12-month breakdown for one adult at 2,000 calories per day, already calculated.

## How much space does a one-year emergency food supply take up?

The 436 pounds of dry goods in this list fill 13 to 15 standard five-gallon food-grade buckets at roughly 33 pounds of rice or 27 to 28 pounds of beans per bucket. Add the canned goods, oil, and miscellaneous items and the total comes to 22 to 25 buckets plus 144 large cans.

Arranged on two rows of 24-by-72-inch wire shelving, a one-year supply for one adult fits in a footprint of about 6 by 4 feet with shelves stacked 5 feet tall. That is the footprint of a standard coat closet. A household of four scales to roughly 25 square feet of organized shelf space, or the equivalent of a small walk-in closet.

This physical estimate is worth doing before you buy anything. Most households have the space; they just have not visualized it in buckets.

## How much does a one-year food supply cost to build?

Built from bulk staples, a one-year supply for one adult costs $600 to $900 all in. That breaks down to $541 to $865 for groceries and $60 to $120 for 22 to 25 food-grade five-gallon buckets, Mylar bags, and oxygen absorbers (a one-time container purchase that serves the full build).

Prebuilt commercial kits for one person range from about $1,200 on the low end for basic calorie coverage to $3,500 for kits with greater variety and better protein ratios. The cost difference comes down to labor and packaging: commercial kits arrive ready to stack, while a bulk-staple system requires 3 to 5 hours to sort, seal, label, and shelve.

For a household of four adults, the bulk approach costs approximately $2,400 to $3,600. Equivalent commercial kit coverage runs $5,000 to $14,000. Most households with available storage space find the cost difference hard to ignore.

Cost per 1,000 calories tells the clearest story: bulk dry staples run roughly $0.80 to $1.40 per 1,000 calories. Commercial freeze-dried emergency kits run $3.00 to $8.00 per 1,000 calories. That is a 3x to 6x markup for convenience and variety.

## How do you store a one-year supply so it holds up?

Long shelf life for dry staples requires four conditions: low oxygen, low moisture, stable cool temperature, and light exclusion. A 5-gallon Mylar bag with a 2,000cc oxygen absorber, heat-sealed with a clothes iron and placed inside a food-grade bucket with a gamma lid, meets all four. A 5-gallon Mylar bag runs $1.00 to $1.50; a 2,000cc oxygen absorber costs around $0.50. The per-bucket cost to upgrade from bucket-only storage to Mylar-plus-O2 is under $2.

The critical temperature target is 55 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit. A 10-degree rise in storage temperature roughly halves effective shelf life for most grains and legumes. A garage that peaks at 95 degrees in August provides far shorter effective storage life than a sealed bucket in a climate-controlled interior room, even if both locations look the same on paper.

Vegetable oil is the shortest-lived item in a one-year supply (2 to 4 years for sealed vegetable oil). Buy 12 lbs at a time, use from the front of the stock, and replace as you go. Do not plan to seal oil in Mylar for multi-decade storage; it does not work.

Canned goods need to stay between 50 and 70 degrees Fahrenheit for best quality. Store them off the floor (concrete wicks moisture), away from exterior walls, and out of any space that regularly exceeds 80 degrees. A can of tuna stored at 90 degrees ages faster than the same can in a cool basement, regardless of what the label date says.

## What nutritional gaps appear specifically at the one-year scale?

Two deficiencies show up in nearly every year-long dry-staple supply that rarely surface in 30-day plans: vitamin C and iodine.

Vitamin C is heat-sensitive and water-soluble. Commercially canned vegetables are processed at high heat, and the remaining vitamin C degrades progressively during storage. By 12 to 18 months, canned vegetables retain a small fraction of the vitamin C present at the time of canning. Freeze-dried vegetables skip the high-heat processing step and retain significantly more vitamin C across years of proper storage. This is why the table above includes 12 lbs of freeze-dried mixed vegetables rather than substituting additional canned goods.

Iodine is the second gap, and it is an easy one to close or miss depending on a single purchasing decision. The table specifies iodized salt for a reason. At roughly 1 lb of salt per month (consistent with 1 to 1.5 teaspoons per day in cooking), iodized salt provides the 150 micrograms of iodine per day an adult requires. Sea salt and non-iodized kosher salt provide almost no iodine. A household that defaults to non-iodized salt across a full year runs a real deficiency risk. The daily multivitamin in the list covers this as a backup, but using iodized salt in cooking is the direct solution.

## How do you scale and manage a one-year supply for a full household?

Scale each table line item by household adult-equivalents: children under 8 count as 0.6; children 8 to 13 count as 0.8; teenagers count as 1.0 to 1.2. For a household of two adults and two school-age children, use a multiplier of 3.2.

FEMA recommends one gallon of water per person per day for drinking and basic sanitation ([FEMA Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/food)). Cooking from a dry-staple menu adds 1 to 2 cups of water per meal. A realistic daily water budget for an adult eating primarily from this supply is 1.5 to 2 gallons. A full 365-day water reserve at that rate is not practical to store for most households. A high-capacity gravity filter rated at 0.1 micron or finer, paired with identified nearby water sources, is the operational solution.

Build the supply in phases. Most households complete a one-year supply in 3 to 4 months by adding two to four line items per grocery trip and sealing each batch as they go. Start with rice, beans, and oats (highest calorie-per-dollar), then move to canned proteins and specialty items. Containers and oxygen absorbers are a one-time purchase that covers the entire build.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does it take to build a one-year emergency food supply?**

Most households finish a one-year supply in 3 to 4 months by buying 2 to 4 items per grocery trip. The sealing and labeling work takes 3 to 5 hours spread across the build period. Starting with rice and beans (cheapest calories per pound) and working outward to canned proteins and freeze-dried items last is the most cost-efficient order.

**Is a one-year emergency food supply the right target for most households?**

FEMA recommends a 72-hour minimum and treats 30 days as a solid personal preparedness baseline. A full year goes well beyond standard guidance. Most people who pursue a one-year supply do so to hedge against extended supply chain disruptions, prolonged job loss, or situations where resupply could be delayed by months. It is a reasonable long-term planning goal, not a starting point.

**Can I buy just a commercial kit instead of building my own?**

Yes. Prebuilt one-year kits for one person from established brands run $1,200 to $3,500 and arrive sealed and ready to store. The tradeoff is cost: you pay 3 to 6 times more per calorie than a bulk-staple approach. Kits also tend to include more variety and seasoned entrees, which matters for households that prioritize ease over economy.

**How do you know if the stored food has gone bad?**

For grains and legumes in sealed Mylar bags, press on the bag. It should lie flat against the contents. A puffed bag has experienced a seal failure and the contents should be used promptly. On opening, off smells (rancid, sour, or musty) indicate quality loss. For canned goods, discard any can that is bulging, rusted through, or that sprays liquid when opened. Never taste food from a can you suspect.

**Do children need a separate food plan within a one-year supply?**

Children under 2 need infant formula or breastmilk, not powdered cow's milk. Children managing food allergies or specific dietary needs require individual adjustments to the standard list. Beyond those cases, the general list scaled by the adult-equivalent multipliers above covers most children adequately. A multivitamin formulated for the child's age group fills the same micronutrient gaps as the adult multivitamin does for adults.
