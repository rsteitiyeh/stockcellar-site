Mountain House freeze-dried meals work well for an emergency food supply. Each pouch needs only hot water to prepare, has a claimed 30-year shelf life, and delivers 400 to 700 calories. Three to four pouches per adult per day covers basic caloric needs. A two-week supply for one adult fits inside a single five-gallon bucket.

The right amount depends on how many people you feed, how much water you can store alongside the food, and whether you are building a 72-hour kit or a longer reserve. Mountain House sells individual pouches, large #10 cans, and pre-assembled supply buckets designed for different planning horizons. Each format has trade-offs worth knowing before you buy.

## What makes Mountain House suitable for long-term emergency food storage?

Mountain House uses nitrogen-flush, oxygen-free packaging that halts the oxidation and moisture that spoil most foods. The pouches and cans are sealed with oxygen absorbers, then purged with nitrogen to drop interior oxygen levels below 2 percent. At that level, the microbial and chemical reactions that degrade food slow to near zero.

Oregon Freeze Dry, the company that produces Mountain House, has been operating since 1963, initially as a supplier to the U.S. military and NASA. That background is visible in the product design. The pouches are stand-up, resealable, and need only one container of hot water. There are no dishes beyond the pouch itself, no stove required beyond a way to boil water, and no refrigeration before or after opening.

The freeze-drying process removes roughly 98 percent of moisture from each meal. That concentration is what makes the pouches calorie-dense at low weight. A single pouch weighing four to six ounces can contain 400 to 700 calories. For emergency storage, this means high caloric value relative to shelf space.

## How long does Mountain House freeze-dried food actually last?

Mountain House offers a 30-year taste guarantee on most pouches and cans, one of the longest shelf-life claims in the freeze-dried category. The company validates this through periodic taste-panel tests on stored product.

The 30-year figure is achievable only under good storage conditions. The key variable is temperature. Freeze-dried food stored consistently below 70 degrees Fahrenheit retains quality far longer than food stored in warmer or fluctuating environments. Storing pouches in a hot attic or garage, where summer temperatures can exceed 90 degrees, may cut usable shelf life to 10 years or less.

Practical storage rules:
- Hold temperature consistently below 70 degrees F. Fluctuations matter almost as much as the average.
- Keep pouches away from direct light. UV exposure degrades packaging over time.
- Store off concrete floors. Moisture wicks through concrete; use pallets or shelving.
- Once a pouch is opened, consume contents within seven days.

The 30-year guarantee is the ceiling under ideal conditions. For planning purposes, 15 to 20 years is a more conservative working estimate that holds across a wider range of real storage environments.

## How many calories does a Mountain House supply provide per day?

This is where buyers often encounter an unexpected gap. A standard emergency food planning benchmark is 2,000 calories per adult per day. The U.S. government's emergency preparedness guidance from FEMA recommends storing enough food for at least three days and ideally two weeks ([FEMA Ready.gov emergency food guidance](https://www.ready.gov/food)). Pre-assembled Mountain House kits sometimes deliver closer to 1,200 to 1,500 calories per person per day because they calculate servings rather than daily totals.

A typical Mountain House pouch labeled as "two servings" holds roughly 480 to 660 calories total. At three pouches per day, an adult gets 1,440 to 1,980 calories. At four pouches per day, most adults reach or exceed 2,000. For physically active emergency situations, such as carrying supplies, walking long distances, or working through weather, caloric needs can rise to 2,500 or more.

The fix is simple: calculate daily calorie targets first, then count pouches backward. Do not rely on kit marketing labels like "3-day supply" or "72-hour kit" to imply adequate calories. Read the total calorie count on the packaging.

Large #10 cans are another option. These hold 10 to 16 servings and cost 20 to 30 percent less per calorie than individual pouches, though they require utensils and a larger preparation container.

## How much water do Mountain House meals require, and why does it matter for planning?

Each Mountain House pouch needs roughly 1.5 to 2 cups (12 to 16 fluid ounces) of water to rehydrate. At four pouches per person per day, that adds up to 6 to 8 cups of cooking water, roughly half a gallon, before counting drinking water.

FEMA's preparedness guidance recommends storing one gallon of water per person per day as a minimum, with at least a two-week supply where feasible ([FEMA Ready.gov emergency kit](https://www.ready.gov/food)). That one gallon covers drinking and basic hygiene. Cooking water sits on top of it. Families who plan water storage around drinking needs and forget food-prep water often run short.

A workable rule for planning: add 0.5 gallons of water per adult per day on top of your base drinking water allocation. That covers Mountain House meal prep with a small margin.

Boiling is not strictly required. Mountain House products can rehydrate with cold water, though the process takes 25 to 30 minutes instead of 8 to 10 minutes, and some products have worse texture. Cold-water rehydration is a useful fallback when fuel is scarce, but the meals are significantly more palatable with hot water.

## How should you rotate a Mountain House emergency food supply?

The 30-year shelf life creates a rotation trap. Buying a bucket and storing it for decades sounds like a plan, but households change faster than freeze-dried food expires.

Open and cook at least one pouch from each variety in your supply once a year. That habit accomplishes several things. First, it confirms the food is still palatable and that no packaging has failed. Second, it lets your household practice preparation on a camp stove or other non-kitchen heat source before an actual emergency. Third, it catches variety fatigue early: a pouch your family disliked in 2024 should not still be sitting unopened in 2030.

If you find pouches that nobody in the household will eat, rotate them out and replace with products that match current preferences. A supply that does not get used is not a supply; it is a liability.

## How does Mountain House compare to other emergency food brands?

Mountain House sits in the mid-to-premium tier of the freeze-dried food market. Competing brands include Augason Farms, ReadyWise, and Wise Company. The differences come down to calorie density per dollar, variety, and packaging format.

Mountain House pouches tend to cost more per calorie than large #10 cans from competitors. The trade-off is convenience: the stand-up pouche format requires no utensils and minimal cleanup, which matters in an actual emergency. Competitor bucket kits often advertise lower per-day cost, but check the daily calorie figures in the fine print. Some kits achieve low prices by packing mostly grains at 200-calorie serving sizes.

On product quality, Mountain House is generally regarded as consistent in taste and texture across its product line. That consistency matters for a long-duration supply: calorie-dense food that people refuse to eat under stress is not useful food.

## What dietary factors apply to a Mountain House emergency supply?

Sodium is high in most Mountain House entrees. A typical two-serving pouch delivers 1,200 to 1,800 milligrams of sodium. Adults managing hypertension or kidney conditions should factor this into their emergency diet plan and supplement with lower-sodium items like dried fruit, nuts, and crackers.

Mountain House products contain common allergens including wheat, milk, and eggs in most entrees. The line is not certified gluten-free across the board, though several individual products carry gluten-free labeling. Because shared manufacturing equipment creates cross-contact risk, anyone with celiac disease should review current allergen documentation directly from the manufacturer before purchasing.

The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service recommends checking sodium, allergen, and nutritional content on all shelf-stable emergency food choices, particularly for household members with chronic health conditions ([USDA FSIS shelf-stable food guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/shelf-stable-food)).

Supplement a freeze-dried meal plan with multivitamins, dried fruit, nuts, and high-fiber crackers. Mountain House products are calorie-dense but low in fiber. A fiber gap becomes uncomfortable after several days and can be addressed cheaply with shelf-stable additions.

## FAQ

**How many Mountain House pouches do I need for a 2-week emergency food supply?**

For one adult targeting 2,000 calories per day, plan on 56 pouches over 14 days, at 4 pouches per day averaging 500 calories each. Multiply by the number of adults in your household and adjust downward for children, who typically need 1,500 to 1,800 calories. Build in a 10 percent buffer. A surplus stores; a shortage doesn't.

**Can Mountain House meals be prepared without heat?**

Yes. Mountain House pouches rehydrate with cold water. Cold-water rehydration takes 25 to 30 minutes compared to 8 to 10 minutes with hot water, and the texture of some products is noticeably worse. The method works and is a useful fallback when fuel is scarce or unavailable. Palatability and eating enjoyment are lower, but the meals are safe and calorie-complete.

**How much does a one-month Mountain House emergency supply cost for one person?**

A 30-day supply at 2,000 calories per day runs roughly $850 to $1,200 in individual pouches at mid-2026 prices. Switching to Mountain House #10 cans (which hold 10 to 16 servings each) reduces that to approximately $600 to $900. Pre-assembled "one-month" kits from Mountain House exist but often target 1,200 to 1,500 calories per day; verify the total calorie count before purchasing any pre-packaged kit.

**Does Mountain House offer gluten-free options for an emergency supply?**

Several Mountain House products carry gluten-free labeling, including specific pasta and entree varieties. The full product line is not certified gluten-free because of shared manufacturing equipment, which creates cross-contact risk. Individuals with celiac disease or serious wheat allergies should review the current Mountain House allergen documentation before buying, as formulations change over time.

**Is the Mountain House 30-year shelf life guarantee reliable?**

The 30-year taste guarantee is a marketing claim backed by in-house taste testing, not a regulatory certification. The underlying preservation method (freeze-drying combined with nitrogen flushing and oxygen-barrier packaging) is well-established and does support shelf life well beyond 10 to 15 years under good conditions. Storage temperature is the main variable: consistent storage below 70 degrees F gets you close to the 30-year ceiling; hot storage spaces cut it significantly. Plan for 15 to 20 years as a conservative figure and store in a climate-controlled space if possible.
