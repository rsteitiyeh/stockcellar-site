A food mill for canning tomatoes works by pressing cooked fruit through a perforated disc that holds back skins and seeds while pulp passes through. You get sauce-ready tomato pulp in one step without blanching, shocking, or peeling tomatoes individually. Cook the tomatoes soft, mill them, acidify each jar with bottled lemon juice, and process.

How well that works depends on disc size, tomato variety, and how thoroughly you cook the fruit. A food mill is not the only way to prep tomatoes for canning, but it handles peeling and seeding in a single mechanical pass that a blender or food strainer cannot replicate in the same motion.

## What Does a Food Mill Do When Canning Tomatoes?

A food mill removes skins and seeds by forcing cooked tomatoes through a perforated disc. The disc acts as a mechanical filter: pulp and juice push through the holes while tougher material stays on top and gets scraped out when you reverse the handle. Unlike a blender, which pulverizes everything together, a food mill separates rather than liquefies.

For canning, this distinction matters. Tomato skins add bitterness to the finished sauce, and seeds contribute an astringent note when broken open. Peeling by hand requires blanching each tomato in boiling water, shocking it in ice water, and slipping the skin off one at a time. That method works but takes significant time on a large batch. A food mill handles 20 to 30 pounds of tomatoes in roughly 40 to 50 minutes of milling, including the time to ladle batches and discard the dry material.

One point that basic canning guides often skip: a food mill does not change the acidification requirement for canned tomatoes. The USDA National Center for Home Food Preservation requires adding bottled lemon juice or citric acid to every jar of home-canned tomatoes regardless of processing method. Tomato pH varies by variety, ripeness, and growing conditions, so added acid standardizes safety. A food mill produces beautifully clean pulp, but the jars still need 1 tablespoon of bottled lemon juice per pint or 2 tablespoons per quart before sealing ([nchfp.uga.edu](https://nchfp.uga.edu/)).

## How Do You Use a Food Mill for Canning Tomatoes?

Start with whole, ripe, unblemished tomatoes. Wash them thoroughly, remove the stem scar, and cut into quarters. No peeling, seeding, or coring needed at this stage. Place the cut tomatoes in a large, heavy-bottomed pot. Add a small amount of water only if the pot is dry at the start. Cook over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until tomatoes are completely soft and falling apart. Pasta types like Roma take closer to 35 minutes; thin-skinned heirlooms may be soft in 20 minutes.

Set the food mill over a large bowl or a second pot. Attach the medium disc as a starting point. Ladle the cooked tomatoes into the mill hopper in batches that leave enough room to turn the handle. Crank clockwise steadily. When the material in the hopper looks dry and the handle turns with little resistance, reverse direction briefly to free trapped pulp, then scrape out the dry skins and seeds and discard them. Repeat until all tomatoes are milled.

The resulting pulp is ready for immediate use as crushed tomatoes or tomato sauce. If you need a thicker paste, simmer the milled pulp over medium heat, stirring frequently, until it reaches your target consistency. Milled tomato pulp reduces faster than raw juice because it starts more concentrated.

Before filling jars, add the bottled lemon juice. Fresh lemon juice is not a substitute because its acidity varies. Bottled juice is standardized to a consistent acidity level, which is what the safety guidance is based on.

## What Disc Size Should You Use for Tomato Sauce?

Most food mills come with two or three interchangeable discs. The disc controls texture and determines how much seed material passes through.

| Disc | Approximate Hole Size | Output Texture | Best Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fine | 1.5 mm | Smooth, nearly seedless pulp | Tomato passata, pizza sauce, paste base |
| Medium | 3 mm | Pulp with soft bits, few seeds | Crushed tomatoes, all-purpose sauce |
| Coarse | 5 mm | Chunky, thick pulp with more seeds | Salsa base, stewed tomatoes |

For a standard smooth canning sauce, start with the medium disc. It moves faster than fine, leaves acceptable texture, and catches most seeds. If you want a cleaner result, run the milled pulp through the fine disc on a second pass. A second pass adds about 10 minutes per large batch but produces noticeably smoother sauce with far fewer seed fragments. For salsa, use the coarse disc to preserve chunk texture.

Very ripe, soft tomatoes pass easily through any disc. Firm or undercooked tomatoes will slow the fine disc to a grind. If the mill requires heavy effort to turn, the tomatoes need more cooking, not more force.

## Can You Use a Food Mill on Raw vs. Cooked Tomatoes?

A food mill requires cooked tomatoes. Raw tomatoes are structurally too firm. Their cell walls have not broken down, so the flesh does not separate cleanly from the skins. Attempting to mill raw tomatoes leaves most of the pulp in the discard pile and strains the equipment.

The minimum cooking level is until tomatoes completely lose their shape and press apart easily with a spoon. For Roma tomatoes at medium ripeness, that typically means 30 to 35 minutes. For very ripe, soft heirlooms, 20 minutes can be enough. There is no harm in cooking longer; thoroughly softened tomatoes mill faster and cleaner than barely softened ones.

A technique some experienced canners use: quarter the tomatoes and place them cut side down in the pot before turning on the heat. The cut surfaces release juice immediately, preventing the bottom layer from scorching while the upper layers heat through. It works well for starting a dry pot and becomes especially useful when milling large batches where you are adding tomatoes in stages.

## How Does a Food Mill Compare to a Food Strainer or Blender?

These three tools produce different results, and using the wrong one for a canning batch creates either extra work or an inferior product.

A food strainer, also called a tomato strainer or suction-base cone strainer, works on raw or minimally cooked tomatoes by pulling them through a cone-shaped screen. It processes large volumes quickly. The limitation is that it starts with less-cooked fruit, which produces thinner, more watery output. Getting a canning-thickness sauce from a food strainer almost always requires a long simmer afterward to cook off the extra liquid. For canners who prefer to simmer sauce for flavor development anyway, a strainer is a reasonable choice. For canners who want the most concentrated output from the milling step itself, a food mill produces a better starting point.

A blender processes everything including skins and seeds. The skins contribute bitterness when blended rather than removed, and the seeds release an astringent quality into the sauce. A blender-based sauce requires straining after blending to remove those elements, which adds a step rather than eliminating one. Blenders also aerate the sauce, giving it a pinkish, frothy appearance that settles but affects the look of the final canned product. For making sauce destined for immediate use, a blender is fast and acceptable. For canning, the food mill produces a cleaner base.

A skilled home canner running a 25-pound batch of Roma tomatoes through a food mill can expect roughly 8 to 9 quarts of sauce-ready pulp at medium-disc setting. The same batch through a food strainer produces 10 to 11 quarts of thinner pulp that needs to be cooked down by about 20 percent to reach the same consistency. The food mill front-loads the concentration work; the strainer back-loads it.

## What Tomato Varieties Work Best for Milling?

Paste tomatoes like Roma (also sold as plum or San Marzano-type) contain more flesh and less water than slicing or beefsteak varieties. A 20-pound batch of Romas through a medium disc produces approximately 8 to 9 quarts of thick pulp with minimal simmering needed. The same weight of beefsteak tomatoes produces looser pulp that benefits from additional simmering before canning.

Heirloom and slicing types work fine in a food mill. The trade-off is yield per pound and post-milling simmer time, not quality. If your garden or local source produces primarily large slicing tomatoes, mill them and plan to simmer the sauce somewhat longer to reach canning consistency.

The NCHFP is explicit on one point that directly applies here: slightly overripe, damaged, or moldy tomatoes should not be used for canning. Overripe tomatoes can have elevated pH even when they look acceptable. Since the acidification step with bottled lemon juice provides a safety margin but does not cover for already-compromised fruit, selecting only firm, ripe, unblemished tomatoes is non-negotiable. Canning is not a way to rescue a bad batch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do you have to cook tomatoes before using a food mill?**

Yes. Raw tomatoes are too firm to mill correctly. The internal cell structure must break down first. Cook them over medium heat until they completely lose their shape, usually 20 to 35 minutes depending on variety and ripeness. Tomatoes that still hold their shape when pressed will jam a fine disc and leave a large portion of the pulp in the discard material.

**Do you still need to add lemon juice when using a food mill for canning tomatoes?**

Yes, without exception. Bottled lemon juice is required for all home-canned tomato products regardless of how the tomatoes were processed. The USDA National Center for Home Food Preservation specifies 1 tablespoon per pint or 2 tablespoons per quart. Tomato pH varies enough by variety and growing season that it cannot be assumed safe without added acid. Fresh lemon juice is not equivalent because its acidity level is inconsistent.

**How long does it take to mill tomatoes for a canning batch?**

For a manual food mill, expect about 45 to 55 minutes of milling time for a 25-pound batch, not counting cooking. That includes ladling batches, turning the handle, reversing to free trapped pulp, and discarding the dry material. Electric food mills cut milling time to roughly 15 to 20 minutes for the same volume. Add 25 to 35 minutes of cooking time, and the total active work for a 25-pound batch runs about 75 to 90 minutes before the sauce is ready to fill jars.

**Can you use a food mill to make tomato paste for canning?**

A food mill produces the base, but making paste also requires long simmering to reduce the pulp to paste consistency. After milling, simmer the pulp uncovered over medium-low heat, stirring frequently, until it reduces by roughly two-thirds. The resulting paste can be canned using tested recipes from the NCHFP, which include specific processing times for paste given its thick density and different heat penetration characteristics.

**Is an electric food mill worth it for canning tomatoes?**

For batches under 20 pounds once or twice a season, a manual food mill is sufficient. For canners regularly processing 40 to 100 pounds of tomatoes at a time, an electric model saves significant time and physical effort. The output quality is the same. The main practical difference is speed and the fact that electric models handle underfed or uneven batches less gracefully than a manually controlled crank.

## Bottom Line

A food mill handles the most time-consuming part of canning tomatoes, skin and seed removal, in a single mechanical pass. Use fully cooked tomatoes, match the disc to your target texture, and add bottled lemon juice to every jar before sealing. Medium disc is the right starting point for all-purpose sauce; fine disc on a second pass adds smoothness worth the extra 10 minutes if you are making pizza sauce or passata.

For tested tomato canning recipes with specific processing times and acidification requirements, the [USDA National Center for Home Food Preservation](https://nchfp.uga.edu/) is the authoritative source for home canners in the United States. Their tomato product section covers whole tomatoes, crushed tomatoes, sauce, paste, juice, and salsa, each with separate processing instructions for water-bath and pressure canning.
