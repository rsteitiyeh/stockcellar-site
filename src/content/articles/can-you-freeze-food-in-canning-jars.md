Yes, you can freeze food in canning jars. Wide-mouth mason jars are the right choice for the freezer; narrow-mouth jars crack at the shoulder when liquid expands. Leave at least one inch of headspace for solid foods and 1.5 inches for liquids, cool the food before it goes in, and you will not lose jars to cracking.

The success or failure of freezing in glass jars comes down to two things: jar shape and headspace. Water-based foods expand roughly 9 percent when they freeze. A wide-mouth jar has straight sides that give expanding contents room to push upward. A narrow-mouth jar has a curved shoulder that traps the expansion and cracks the glass. Every other rule in this guide follows from that basic geometry.

## Which Canning Jars Can Go in the Freezer?

Wide-mouth jars are safe for the freezer; regular-mouth (narrow-mouth) jars are not recommended for liquid contents. The shoulder curve on a regular-mouth jar is where the glass breaks when liquid inside freezes and expands. The same jar holds water-bath processed pickles without issue, because glass is not under stress at room temperature. At freezing temperatures, expansion pressure finds the weakest point, and that is always the shoulder on a narrow-mouth jar.

Ball and Kerr label some of their wide-mouth pint and half-pint jars as "freezer safe." Half-pint (8 oz) wide-mouth jars are particularly practical because smaller portions freeze and thaw faster, reducing the time food spends at partial-thaw temperatures. Pint wide-mouth jars work for soups, stocks, and sauces meant to be used in a single meal.

Half-gallon jars, even wide-mouth, are not a good freezer choice. The thermal mass is too large. The outside freezes while the center is still liquid, creating uneven pressure and a much higher cracking risk than a pint jar of the same contents.

## How Do You Freeze Food in Mason Jars Without Them Breaking?

The breaking mechanism is thermal shock combined with expansion pressure. Avoid both and the jars survive.

Start with cool food. Do not pour boiling soup directly into a mason jar and immediately put it in the freezer. Let hot food cool on the counter for 20 to 30 minutes, then refrigerate until it reaches 40 degrees Fahrenheit or below before moving it to the freezer. This step prevents thermal shock to the glass and reduces the expansion event because the food is already cold when it freezes solid.

Leave the right amount of headspace. For liquids with high water content, leave 1.5 inches at the top. For chunky foods where liquid is not the dominant component, one inch is usually enough. Fill the jar no higher than the freeze-fill line printed on Ball freezer jars. The expansion has to go somewhere; the headspace is where it goes. A quart jar (32 oz) at a 1.5-inch headspace has roughly 2.9 ounces of room, which covers the 9 percent volume expansion of water-based contents at standard fill levels.

Freeze jars upright, not on their side. An upright jar lets the food expand straight up into the headspace. A jar on its side redirects that expansion against the glass wall or lid, creating unpredictable stress on the glass.

Leave the lid slightly loose until the contents freeze solid, then tighten it. This allows air to escape rather than building pressure during the initial freeze. Once the jar is fully frozen (plan on 12 hours minimum for a pint), tighten the lid to prevent freezer burn during storage.

Avoid stacking glass jars. Unlike plastic containers, jars do not flex under weight. Stack pressure on a frozen jar can crack it, particularly at the seam near the lid.

## What Foods Freeze Well in Canning Jars?

Soups and stocks are the strongest use case. A quart jar of chicken stock or a pint of vegetable soup fits the freezer efficiently, defrosts in the refrigerator overnight, and glass does not absorb odors the way plastic containers do over months of use. Stocks hold quality for three to four months at 0 degrees Fahrenheit.

Tomato sauce and chili work well in pint jars. Fill to the freeze line, cool completely in the refrigerator first, then freeze. Both expand moderately and defrost easily with an overnight refrigerator thaw or a room-temperature water bath.

Fruit purees, smoothie packs, and portioned baby food freeze well in half-pint jars. The smaller volume means faster freezing and less risk of the center staying liquid while the outside is already frozen.

Dry goods are where canning jars provide a benefit that surprises most people. Freezing flour, cornmeal, nuts, and whole grains in sealed mason jars kills any insect eggs or larvae present in the product at the time of purchase. Hold dry goods at 0 degrees Fahrenheit for four full days to achieve this result, then move them to pantry storage. There is no expansion concern with dry goods, no headspace required, and the jars can be any style.

Cooked beans freeze well and defrost with the right texture. A cup of cooked beans in a half-pint jar substitutes directly for a can of beans and holds its texture better in glass than in thin freezer bags, which can develop ice crystals on the surface.

## What Foods Should You Not Freeze in Canning Jars?

Carbonated beverages and anything fermented that is still actively producing gas will crack jars under the combined pressure of gas and expansion. Do not freeze active kombucha or lacto-fermented vegetables still at peak fermentation in glass jars.

Raw egg yolks do not freeze well in any container. The yolk becomes gummy on thawing and does not recover. Beaten whole eggs and egg whites freeze normally in mason jars, however.

High-fat dairy presents a different issue. Cream-based soups and sauces with heavy cream freeze without cracking the jar, but the fat separates on thawing. The glass is not the problem; the food itself does not survive the freeze-thaw cycle with the texture it had going in. If you freeze cream-based soups, plan to re-emulsify them with a quick whisk on reheating.

Produce with very high water content, like cucumbers, raw cabbage, and iceberg lettuce, does not improve in the freezer under any container. Ice crystals rupture cell walls and the result is mushy. This is a food quality problem, not a jar problem.

Large raw meat cuts do not belong in mason jars because the geometry is awkward and inefficient. Stiff freezer bags or purpose-built containers handle those better. Cooked and shredded meat freezes fine in wide-mouth pints.

## How Much Headspace Do You Leave When Freezing in Mason Jars?

Headspace is the air gap between the surface of the food and the underside of the lid. The National Center for Home Food Preservation recommends 0.5 to 1.5 inches of headspace when freezing food in glass containers, depending on food type. For practical purposes, use these as your working numbers:

Liquids and high-liquid foods (soups, sauces, stocks, fruit juices): 1.5 inches. Water expands approximately 9 percent by volume when it freezes. A quart jar filled to the 1.5-inch headspace mark has enough room to absorb that expansion without pressure against the lid.

Chunky solids with some liquid (beans, chili, fruit in syrup): one inch. The solid portion does not expand as dramatically as pure liquid, and a smaller headspace handles the event adequately.

Dry goods (flour, cornmeal, nuts, grains): no headspace required. No expansion happens, so fill to the shoulder or neck of the jar.

If you are unsure which category your food falls into, 1.5 inches is always the safe default. Over-filling is what breaks jars. Under-filling slightly costs you a little storage space and nothing else.

## Can You Put Mason Jars Directly from the Freezer into Hot Water?

No. Moving a frozen mason jar directly into boiling water is thermal shock, and it breaks glass reliably. A mason jar, even a tempered one, is not engineered for rapid temperature changes. The glass expands and contracts at different rates in different zones when heated suddenly, and that differential stress cracks it.

The right defrost approach is an overnight refrigerator thaw or a cool water bath. To speed thawing in water, use water at room temperature and change it every 30 minutes. This brings a pint jar of soup to a liquid state in about an hour without crack risk.

Alternatively, set the frozen jar on the counter and allow it to reach room temperature before applying any heat. A quart jar of soup takes two to three hours on the counter. Once the contents are no longer frozen solid, transfer to a pot for reheating.

Microwave thawing works with glass. Remove the lid, use a low power setting with short intervals, and stir between cycles to distribute heat evenly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can regular-mouth mason jars be used in the freezer?**

Regular-mouth mason jars can store dry goods in the freezer without issue since there is no liquid expansion. For soups, sauces, or any water-based content, stick with wide-mouth jars. The shoulder curve on regular-mouth jars creates a stress point where expanding liquid breaks the glass. Wide-mouth jars have straighter sidewalls that let expansion travel upward into the headspace instead.

**Do I need to leave the lid off while the jar freezes?**

No. Leave the lid on but tighten it only loosely until the jar is fully frozen. A slightly loose lid lets air escape during the initial freeze without building pressure inside the jar. Once the contents are frozen solid (12 hours or more for a pint), tighten the lid fully to prevent freezer burn during longer storage.

**How long can food stay frozen in mason jars?**

The timeline is the same as for any sealed freezer container. Soups and stocks hold quality for three to four months at 0 degrees Fahrenheit. Cooked beans hold quality for about six months. Fruit purees hold quality for eight to twelve months. The jar material does not shorten these windows. Safety extends well beyond them, but quality (texture, flavor) declines with time.

**Can you reuse canning jars after freezing?**

Yes, with inspection. Before returning a jar to canning use, check it carefully for hairline cracks, especially at the shoulder and along the sidewall seam. A jar that has been through the freezer without visible cracking is safe to use again. Freeze damage, when it happens, is usually obvious as a visible crack through the sidewall. The lid and band should also be inspected; lids are single-use for canning regardless of whether the jar was ever frozen.

**Why do mason jars sometimes crack in the freezer even with headspace?**

Usually the cause is thermal shock rather than insufficient headspace. A jar that goes from the stove into the freezer without cooling down first will crack from the rapid temperature change even if the headspace is correct. The fix is to cool food to refrigerator temperature before freezing. A secondary cause is jar style: if any cracking happens at the shoulder curve, the jar is a narrow-mouth jar being used for liquid contents.

## Bottom Line

Wide-mouth canning jars are reliable freezer containers when you follow two rules: leave adequate headspace (1.5 inches for liquids, one inch for chunky solids) and cool the food to refrigerator temperature before it goes in the freezer. Narrow-mouth jars crack at the shoulder under liquid expansion and should be reserved for dry goods only. Glass is heavier than plastic, but it does not absorb odors, does not leach compounds under temperature stress, and lasts for decades with basic care.

For complete freezing guidelines and recommended headspace by food type, the [USDA National Center for Home Food Preservation](https://nchfp.uga.edu/) is the authoritative reference. Ball's usage guidelines for their jar lines are available at [ballmasonjars.com](https://www.ballmasonjars.com/).
