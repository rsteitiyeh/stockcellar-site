Emergency supply food should give every person in your household several days of familiar, shelf-stable meals that can be eaten safely with little water, fuel, or preparation. Start with a three-day menu, add the matching groceries and water, then extend the plan as space and budget allow.

## How much emergency food should you store?

Store at least a three-day supply, then work toward a longer reserve that fits the hazards and likely service disruptions where you live. FEMA's [basic emergency supply kit checklist](https://www.ready.gov/sites/default/files/documents/files/checklist3.pdf) calls for at least three days of nonperishable food and one gallon of water per person per day for drinking and sanitation.

A meal-based plan is more dependable than counting cans. Write down breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks for each day and each person. Then total the actual packages required. A four-person household planning oatmeal for three breakfasts, for example, needs 12 servings plus the water and heat needed to prepare them.

Use package Nutrition Facts and serving directions to judge portions. Needs vary with age, body size, activity, health, temperature, and stress. Include extra food when someone is pregnant, nursing, ill, or doing strenuous cleanup. Follow a clinician's advice for medically prescribed diets.

This table offers a planning framework, not a universal nutrition prescription:

| Planning period | What to assemble | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| 3 days | Complete meals, snacks, and required water for each person | First target for outages and short disruptions |
| 7 days | A second menu cycle with more variety | A practical next step for many homes |
| 14 days | Two weekly menus plus extra no-cook choices | Added resilience when roads or stores may stay closed |

Count pets separately. Store their usual food, needed medicines, a bowl, and water. Sudden diet changes can upset an animal, especially during an already stressful event.

## Which foods belong in an emergency supply?

Choose foods that keep at room temperature, require little preparation, and already suit your household's tastes and health needs. Ready-to-eat canned meat, fish, beans, fruit, and vegetables can form the center of meals. Round them out with nut or seed butter, crackers, dry cereal, shelf-stable milk, fruit cups, energy bars, and dried fruit.

Variety matters because people may eat less when every meal feels the same. Pair foods into simple combinations before buying large quantities. Tuna with crackers, beans with canned corn, or cereal with shelf-stable milk are easier to picture and portion than an unplanned shelf of assorted cans.

Favor packages that one meal can finish. Once opened, a large can may need refrigeration, and leftovers can become unsafe during a power outage. Choose pop-top cans when practical, but keep a manual can opener in the kit. Add disposable plates or bowls, utensils, paper towels, and trash bags if water for washing dishes may be limited.

Check every label for preparation requirements. Dry rice, pasta, and beans store well, yet they demand water and cooking fuel. They can support a longer pantry plan, but a short-term kit should also contain foods that are safe to eat straight from the package. Never use a charcoal grill, camp stove, or generator inside a home or garage.

## How should food allergies and special diets change the plan?

Build the menu around each person's daily restrictions rather than treating special foods as optional extras. Read labels whenever you rotate stock because ingredients and manufacturing statements can change.

For infants, store the formula they normally use and follow the label's preparation instructions. Ready-to-feed formula can reduce dependence on safe mixing water, though it may cost more and take more space. Keep suitable bottles, nipples, cleaning supplies, and any feeding equipment together.

For diabetes, kidney disease, swallowing problems, or other medical needs, ask the treating clinician or dietitian what shelf-stable substitutes are suitable. Store written instructions with the kit. People who have trouble chewing may do better with soft canned fruit, smooth nut or seed butter, ready-to-eat pudding, and other familiar textures, provided those foods meet their dietary requirements.

Do not forget religious and cultural food practices. A technically adequate meal that someone will refuse does little good. Let household members taste candidate foods during an ordinary week before purchasing a case.

## How much water should you keep with the food?

Keep at least one gallon of water per person per day for three days, and try to build toward two weeks when storage permits. The CDC's [emergency water supply guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-create-and-store-an-emergency-water-supply.html) says this water covers drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, and other uses. It also advises storing more for hot climates, illness, pregnancy, and pets.

Water is part of the food plan. Add the amount needed for oatmeal, powdered drinks, soup, and any dehydrated meals to your calculations. When water is scarce, serve canned food with edible liquid, such as fruit packed in juice, and prioritize ready-to-eat items.

Unopened commercial bottled water is the CDC's safest and most reliable emergency option. Observe the printed date and protect bottles from heat and chemicals. If you fill your own containers, use food-grade water containers and follow CDC cleaning, sanitizing, and replacement directions. Never use a container that previously held toxic chemicals.

## Where and how should supplies be stored?

Store emergency food in a cool, dry, accessible place away from household chemicals, pests, and flood risk. Keep packages off the floor and protect dry goods in sturdy food-safe containers when insects or rodents are a concern.

Write the purchase date and the package's best-by or use-by date where you can see them. Place items with earlier dates at the front. During a scheduled check every six months, inspect for leaks, rust, deep dents, swelling, broken seals, moisture, pests, and unusual odors. Discard unsafe or questionable packages according to local waste guidance.

A best-by date usually concerns quality, but package condition and storage history still matter. Follow any use-by date and manufacturer directions. Rotate ordinary pantry foods through regular meals and replace them during routine shopping. This keeps the reserve familiar and spreads the cost over time.

Keep a small evacuation set in a handled bin or backpack. Favor lightweight, ready-to-eat packages and include water, utensils, a can opener, medication-related snacks, and a written inventory. The larger home supply can remain on sturdy shelving.

## What should you do when the power goes out?

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed, use shelf-stable food first when practical, and track how long the power has been off. USDA food-safety guidance says an unopened refrigerator keeps food safe for up to four hours; a full freezer holds temperature for about 48 hours, or about 24 hours when half full. An appliance thermometer helps you make safer decisions.

The USDA's [emergency food safety instructions](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/emergencies/keep-your-food-safe-during-emergencies) say never to taste food to decide whether it is safe. Perishable refrigerated foods should be discarded after four hours without power unless they have been kept at 40°F or below. Frozen food may be refrozen or cooked if it still contains ice crystals or measures 40°F or below.

After a flood, discard food that contacted floodwater unless official guidance specifically identifies the sealed container as salvageable and explains how to sanitize it. Throw away packages that are open, damaged, leaking, bulging, or spurting liquid. When local authorities issue a boil-water or do-not-use notice, follow their directions for cooking and cleanup.

## How can you build the supply without overspending?

Build one tested day of food at a time and buy duplicates during normal grocery trips. Start with meals your household already eats, compare unit prices, and avoid a large specialty purchase until everyone has sampled it.

Use a paper inventory or a note on your phone with the item, quantity, location, and date. Set two calendar reminders each year to inspect supplies, replace water as needed, review dietary changes, and test the manual can opener. Eat foods approaching their dates and put fresh replacements at the back.

The finished reserve should function as a menu, not a collection. If every item has a planned meal, enough safe water, a way to open it, and a person willing and able to eat it, your [emergency food supply](/emergency-food-supply/) is ready to help when normal cooking and shopping stop.
