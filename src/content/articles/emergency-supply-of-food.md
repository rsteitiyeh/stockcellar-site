An emergency supply of food should give every person in your household several days of familiar, shelf-stable meals that can be eaten with little water, cooking, or refrigeration. Start with at least three days, add safe drinking water and essential tools, then expand the supply as your space and budget allow.

## How much emergency food should you store?

Store at least three days of nonperishable food for every household member, then work toward a longer buffer if you can manage it. FoodSafety.gov advises households to plan for [at least a three-day food supply](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency) because a disaster may interrupt normal access to groceries.

Use person-days to scale that guidance. Multiply the number of people by the number of days you want to cover. A four-person household planning for seven days needs 28 person-days of food. Count complete daily menus rather than cans or boxes, since package sizes and appetites vary.

Write one realistic day of meals for each person, including snacks and drinks. Then multiply that menu by your target number of days. Adjust for children, older adults, pregnancy, allergies, medical diets, cultural preferences, and anyone who may need help opening packages or preparing food. Keep pet food in the same plan, but separate from the human-food count.

Water belongs beside the food. The CDC recommends [at least one gallon per person per day for three days](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-create-and-store-an-emergency-water-supply.html) and suggests trying to store a two-week supply when possible. More may be needed for hot weather, illness, pregnancy, or pets. This water supports drinking, cooking, and hygiene, so dry foods that need a great deal of water may strain the supply.

## What foods belong in an emergency supply?

Choose foods your household already eats that stay safe at room temperature and require little fuel or water. A useful stock includes ready-to-eat proteins, fruits or vegetables, filling starches, compact snacks, and drinks or foods that support special dietary needs.

| Food role | Practical choices | What to check before buying |
|---|---|---|
| Ready-to-eat protein | Canned beans, canned fish or chicken, nut or seed butter | Allergies, sodium, pull-top or can-opener needs |
| Fruit and vegetables | Canned fruit, applesauce cups, canned vegetables, dried fruit | Package size, added sugar, liquid that can be consumed |
| Starches | Crackers, tortillas, ready-to-eat rice pouches, dry cereal | Water and heat needed, package durability |
| Quick energy | Granola bars, trail mix, shelf-stable pudding | Heat sensitivity, nuts, choking concerns |
| Comfort and variety | Instant coffee, tea, cocoa mix, familiar seasonings | Water and fuel needed |
| Special needs | Infant formula, medically appropriate foods, texture-modified foods | Exact user instructions and current dates |

Build menus before shopping. One sample no-cook day might pair cereal with shelf-stable milk for breakfast, crackers and canned fish for lunch, and beans with ready-to-eat rice for dinner. Fruit cups and nut butter can fill gaps. This is only a pattern; select portions and foods that fit the people who will eat them.

Favor smaller packages when refrigeration may fail. Once a can, jar, or carton is opened, leftovers may become perishable. Single-meal sizes can reduce waste, although they often cost more and create more packaging. Balance those drawbacks against the chance that you will have no safe way to chill an opened container.

Avoid making the entire supply salty or spicy. Federal guidance notes that these foods can increase the need for drinking water. Include a few foods that can be eaten straight from the package, even if your usual plan relies on a camp stove or other cooking method.

## How should emergency food be stored and rotated?

Store emergency food in a cool, dry, dark place, inspect it regularly, and replace each item before the date or condition makes it unsuitable. FoodSafety.gov identifies 40°F to 70°F as the best temperature range for canned and dried emergency foods and recommends keeping them away from heat, petroleum products, pests, and possible floodwater.

Put the oldest items at the front and newer purchases behind them. Add emergency foods to ordinary meals, then replace what you use. This first-in, first-out routine limits waste and reveals whether the household actually likes the products in storage.

Mark a review date on the outside of the bin or cabinet. At each review:

1. Check package dates and follow the manufacturer's storage directions.
2. Look for leaks, swelling, deep dents, rust, broken seals, moisture, or pest damage.
3. Confirm that dietary needs and household size have not changed.
4. Replace foods that are near their planned use-by point.
5. Update the paper inventory and sample menu.

Do not use a fixed replacement schedule for every product. Commercial foods have different date labels and storage requirements. Follow the label, keep an inventory, and rotate items while they are still foods you would willingly serve.

Place food above likely flood level and away from cleaners, gasoline, paint, and other chemicals. Paper boxes and thin bags benefit from a clean, airtight outer container that discourages insects and rodents. Keep food in its original package inside that container so preparation directions, ingredients, allergen warnings, and lot information remain available.

## What tools should you keep with the food?

Keep a manual can opener, clean utensils, a food thermometer, lighting, and a safe method for preparing any food that requires heat. A printed menu and inventory reduce guesswork when power and internet service are unavailable.

If your plan includes cooking, use only equipment approved for the location where you operate it. Grills and many fuel-burning camp stoves can produce carbon monoxide and must not be used inside a home, garage, tent, or other enclosed area. A safer food plan always includes meals that require no cooking.

Also store disposable plates or bowls if water for washing will be scarce, along with paper towels and trash bags. Include a permanent marker for dating opened packages and relabeling items if an outer label is damaged. Put needed feeding supplies with infant, disability-related, or pet foods.

## How do you use stored food during a power outage?

Use shelf-stable food first when doing so lets you keep the refrigerator and freezer closed. According to FoodSafety.gov, an unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours, while a closed full freezer holds temperature for about 48 hours and a half-full freezer for about 24 hours.

An appliance thermometer helps you judge conditions. Refrigerated perishable foods such as meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and leftovers should be discarded after four hours without power. Frozen food may be cooked or refrozen if it still has ice crystals or measures 40°F or below. Never taste questionable food to test its safety.

Food exposed to floodwater requires special caution. Discard food in packaging that is not waterproof, along with damaged, bulging, leaking, or opened containers. Follow official local instructions after a flood or other contamination event; appearance alone cannot show whether food is safe.

## How can you build the supply on a budget?

Build it one ordinary grocery trip at a time, starting with a complete three-day menu. Buy an extra can, pouch, or box of a food your household uses, record it, and repeat until every meal is covered.

Prioritize function before variety: cover water, daily meals, special diets, and no-cook options first. Compare unit prices, but check whether a larger package would create unsafe leftovers during an outage. Store brands and sale purchases can work well when the household likes the food and the package will remain within its date through your planned rotation cycle.

Test one emergency meal during a normal week. Note how much water, fuel, time, and cleanup it requires. Replace awkward choices before an emergency exposes the problem. A modest, familiar supply that gets checked and eaten is dependable because it remains current and matched to your household.
