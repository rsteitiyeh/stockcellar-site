A ReadyWise emergency food supply can be a useful compact layer in a home preparedness plan, especially when pantry space and shelf life matter. Buy by calories and actual days of food, though, rather than relying on the serving count printed on the bucket.

ReadyWise sells several configurations, so there is no single formula that describes every bucket. Most emergency kits combine dehydrated or freeze-dried ingredients in sealed pouches, and many meals require water plus a way to heat it. That makes the food convenient to store, but it does not make the bucket a complete emergency kit.

## Is a ReadyWise emergency food supply worth buying?

It can be worth buying if you want packaged meals with long labeled storage lives and have already planned for water, cooking, and dietary needs. It is less attractive when you want food that can be eaten immediately, single-person portions, or familiar groceries that you routinely rotate.

The main benefit is density. A bucket groups many shelf-stable meals in a format that fits on a closet shelf and is easier to move than the same number of assorted cans. The tradeoff is dependence on preparation resources. If the tap stops running or the stove is unavailable, a pouch of dry pasta contributes little until you can add safe water and cook it as directed.

Serving counts also need context. For example, ReadyWise's official page for its 120-serving bucket lists 24,540 total calories and describes that as 13 days at 1,800 calories per day. It also says the bucket needs about 120 cups of water and that its pouches contain six servings. Those figures are more useful than the large number on the front label because they show the energy, water, and portioning constraints in the same kit. See the current [ReadyWise 120-serving product specifications](https://readywise.com/products/120-serving-emergency-food-supply) before purchasing because recipes and package contents can change.

| Label or specification | What it means for planning |
|---|---|
| 120 servings | A unit count, not 120 person-days |
| 24,540 calories | About 13.6 days at 1,800 calories per day by simple division |
| About 120 cups of water | Roughly 7.5 gallons needed for preparation |
| Six servings per pouch | Opening one pouch may create leftovers for a solo user |
| Up to 25-year shelf life | A maximum claim tied to the product and suitable storage conditions |

Those calculations describe that specific bucket, not every ReadyWise product. Breakfast, fruit, vegetable, protein, and mixed-meal buckets can have very different calorie totals. Always compare the current nutrition panels and product specifications for the exact SKU you are considering.

## How many buckets do you actually need?

Calculate your household's target calories for the planned number of days, then compare that number with each bucket's total calories. A four-person household planning for three days has 12 person-days to cover. The correct bucket count depends on the household's needs and on what other food is already in the pantry.

Federal guidance offers a sensible minimum starting point. The [Ready.gov basic emergency supply checklist](https://www.ready.gov/sites/default/files/documents/files/checklist3.pdf) calls for at least a three-day supply of nonperishable food and one gallon of water per person per day for at least three days. That water allotment covers drinking and sanitation. Water needed to rehydrate meals must come from the same finite household supply, so it belongs in your calculation.

Use this process before ordering:

1. Count every person who will rely on the supply, including anyone who may stay with you during a storm.
2. Choose the number of days you want to cover, starting with the government minimum and adjusting for local hazards.
3. Estimate daily calorie needs for each person based on age, body size, health, and activity.
4. Total the calories in the exact kit, using its nutrition labels rather than its marketing name.
5. Subtract the calories already available in dependable shelf-stable pantry food.
6. Confirm that stored water covers drinking, meal preparation, and reasonable sanitation needs.

Do not treat 1,800 calories as a universal requirement. A child's needs differ from an adult's, and strenuous cleanup work can raise energy demands. People who manage diabetes, kidney disease, food allergies, or medically prescribed diets should review the ingredient and nutrition panels with an appropriate health professional.

## What should you check before buying?

Check calories, ingredients, allergens, sodium, water requirements, pouch size, preparation directions, and the date coding on the exact package. This short audit catches most of the ways a promising bucket can fail to match a household.

ReadyWise identifies milk, soy, and wheat as common allergens in its emergency foods, while individual recipes vary. A variety bucket may include only some items that fit a particular restriction. Phrases such as "gluten free" on selected meals do not mean every pouch in the bucket has that attribute.

Nutrition deserves the same attention. Emergency entrees often function best as a base to which you add ordinary pantry foods. Canned fish, nut or seed butter, shelf-stable milk, canned fruit, and ready-to-eat beans can add energy or variety without requiring every meal to come from one bucket. Choose additions your household already eats and can safely store.

Pouch size affects waste. A six-serving pouch works well for a family but may be awkward for one person during an outage, especially if refrigeration is unavailable. Once you open and prepare a pouch, follow its label for handling leftovers. The USDA warns that an unopened refrigerator keeps food safely cold for about four hours during a power outage; its [emergency food-safety guidance](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/emergencies/keep-your-food-safe-during-emergencies) also says never to taste food to judge whether it is safe.

## How should you store and use the food?

Store the sealed food in a cool, dry, stable location and inspect it on a regular schedule. ReadyWise says heat, moisture, oxygen, and light affect its products, and describes a cool, dry place as optimal. A hot attic, damp basement, or vehicle with large temperature swings is a poor long-term location even when a bucket physically fits there.

Keep the bucket off bare concrete and above any plausible flood level. Avoid shelves beside a furnace, water heater, dryer vent, or sunny window. Record the purchase date, printed lot or best-by information, and contents in a simple inventory. Inspect the container for cracks, pests, moisture, broken seals, or swollen pouches. Follow the package instructions when deciding whether aging or damaged food should be discarded.

Then test a pouch well before an emergency. This reveals whether your family likes the meal, how much fuel it takes, whether the texture benefits from extra standing time, and which pot is the right size. Replace the test pouch so the planned calorie total remains intact.

Keep a printed preparation note with the bucket and store the necessary equipment nearby: a manual can opener for companion foods, a measured container, utensils, and a safe backup cooking method. Never operate a fuel-burning camp stove or grill indoors. The kit should still include some no-cook foods for evacuation, illness, or any event that leaves you without safe cooking facilities.

## What is the best way to build a complete emergency menu?

Use ReadyWise meals as one layer alongside water, no-cook staples, and routinely rotated pantry food. This approach reduces dependence on any single package and gives you options when time, fuel, or water is limited.

A practical three-day menu rehearsal is more revealing than a serving count. Set aside the food and water you expect to use, prepare it with your backup equipment, and note what runs short. Include drinks, snacks, condiments, infant supplies, pet food, and any medically necessary nutrition. If the trial exposes too much sodium, too little protein, or repeated meals no one wants, adjust the pantry now.

The final buying decision is straightforward: choose a ReadyWise bucket only after its calories, labels, preparation needs, and pouch sizes fit your household plan. Stored correctly and backed by water plus no-cook food, it can fill a useful role. By itself, the bucket contains only dry food and leaves major preparedness needs uncovered.
