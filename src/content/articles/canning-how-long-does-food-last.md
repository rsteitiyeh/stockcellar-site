For canning, how long does food last? Properly processed home-canned food should be used within one year for best quality, while unopened commercial canned food generally keeps its best quality for 12 months to 5 years, depending on acidity.

The date alone cannot prove that a jar or can is safe. Processing method, an intact seal, container condition, and storage temperature all matter. When you stock a preparedness pantry, use the recommended time as a rotation target and inspect every container before opening it.

## How long does home-canned food last?

Home-canned food keeps its optimum eating quality for at least one year when it was processed correctly and stored in a cool, dry place. The [National Center for Home Food Preservation's canning FAQ](https://nchfp.uga.edu/faqs/general-canning/category/faq-canning) gives that one-year quality benchmark.

Treat 12 months as your practical use-by goal. That does not mean a correctly canned jar becomes unsafe on day 366. Quality gradually falls, and the chance of a failed seal or storage damage grows with time. Color may fade or darken, liquid can cloud, and texture can soften even when the seal remains sound.

The one-year guidance assumes you started with a current, research-tested recipe and followed its jar size, ingredient proportions, processing method, processing time, and altitude adjustment. A tight lid cannot make an untested or underprocessed recipe safe. If you do not know how a home-canned food was processed, age and appearance cannot settle the safety question.

## How long do commercial canned foods last?

Commercially canned high-acid foods retain their best quality for 12 to 18 months, while low-acid foods generally retain it for 2 to 5 years. These ranges apply to unopened cans in good condition stored in a cool, clean, dry place, according to the [USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/food-product-dating).

High-acid foods include tomatoes, pineapple, grapefruit, and many other fruits. Low-acid foods include meat, poultry, fish, and most vegetables. Acid reacts with the container over time, so high-acid products tend to lose flavor and texture sooner.

Most dates on shelf-stable cans indicate peak quality rather than a hard safety cutoff. Infant formula is a separate product with federally regulated use-by dating and should never be treated as an ordinary canned pantry item. Always follow package directions for products labeled "Keep Refrigerated."

| Canned food category | Unopened storage target | After opening |
|---|---:|---:|
| Home-canned food | Use within 12 months for best quality | Refrigerate and use within 3 to 4 days |
| Commercial high-acid food | 12 to 18 months for best quality | Refrigerate and use within 5 to 7 days |
| Commercial low-acid food | 2 to 5 years for best quality | Refrigerate and use within 3 to 4 days |

The opened-food times come from the USDA's [guidance for refrigerated canned leftovers](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/After-you-open-a-can-how-long-can-you-keep-the-food-in-the-refrigerator). Transfer leftovers to a clean glass or plastic container if you want better flavor, cover them, and refrigerate promptly. Commercially canned ham and seafood may have special label instructions, so the package takes priority over a general chart.

## Does a sealed jar stay safe forever?

No, a seal alone does not guarantee that home-canned food was safely prepared or remains fit to eat. A lid can seal after an unsafe process, and a sound seal can later fail because of corrosion, heat, residue, or physical damage.

For home canning, acid level determines the safe method. Low-acid vegetables, meat, poultry, and seafood require a pressure canner because boiling-water temperatures do not destroy botulism spores. Acid foods can be processed with a tested boiling-water or atmospheric-steam canning procedure. Tomatoes require the acidification and method specified by the tested recipe.

Use recipes from USDA, the [National Center for Home Food Preservation](/national-center-for-home-food-preservation/), or a state Extension service. Old family instructions may omit pressure canning, altitude adjustments, or tested processing times. Extra boiling after opening does not repair a questionable canning process.

## What storage conditions give canned food the longest life?

A clean, dark, dry space between 50 and 70 degrees F gives home-canned food its best chance of retaining quality. The [National Center for Home Food Preservation storage guidance](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/can/general-information/storing-home-canned-foods/) also says not to store jars above 95 degrees F or near a range, furnace, hot pipes, or direct sunlight.

Heat speeds quality loss and can contribute to spoilage. Moisture encourages rust on commercial cans and corrosion on jar lids. Freezing can soften food and may break a jar or disturb its seal, although freezing by itself does not spoil food if the container remains sealed.

Prepare home-canned jars for storage this way:

1. Let processed jars cool undisturbed for 12 to 24 hours.
2. Test each seal according to the lid manufacturer's directions.
3. Remove screw bands, then wash and dry the jars and lids.
4. Label each jar with the food and canning date.
5. Store jars upright in a cool, dark, dry cabinet or shelf.
6. Put the oldest sound jars at the front and use them first.

Keeping ring bands off during storage makes a broken seal easier to spot and prevents a loose lid from being held in place. It also reduces trapped moisture around the band. Avoid tall, unsupported stacks that could disturb the seals on lower jars.

## How can you tell when canned food should be discarded?

Discard canned food without tasting it when the container leaks, bulges, has lost its seal, or shows other signs of spoilage. Botulinum toxin cannot be reliably detected by sight, smell, or taste, so a normal-looking product does not excuse an unsafe or unknown home-canning method.

Check a home-canned jar before moving it from the shelf. The lid should be concave and held firmly by vacuum. Look for dried food streaks from the rim, rising bubbles, unnatural color, or mold. As you open the jar, watch for spurting liquid and notice any unnatural odor. Never taste food to decide whether it is safe.

For commercial cans, reject containers that are swollen, leaking, badly rusted, or deeply dented, especially along a seam. A small cosmetic dent away from seams differs from a sharp or deep dent that may have damaged the can. When the condition is uncertain, discarding the food is the safer choice.

Suspect home-canned low-acid food needs careful handling because contact with botulinum toxin can be fatal. Do not open a swollen or leaking jar just to investigate it. Follow the National Center for Home Food Preservation's [instructions for identifying and handling spoiled canned food](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/can/generalinformation/identifying-and-handling-spoiled-canned-food/) rather than improvising disposal or cleanup.

## How should a long-term pantry rotation work?

An annual home-canning cycle and a first-in, first-out shelf system keep food within its best-quality window. Write the month and year where you can see them without moving every jar, and keep a simple inventory by food type and quantity.

Plan to finish last season's home-canned goods before the same crop returns. For commercial cans, sort by the package's best-quality date while keeping the USDA ranges in mind. Inspect shelves every few months for moisture, pests, corrosion, broken seals, and temperature problems.

Buy or can amounts your household actually eats. A five-year commercial storage range offers little value if the food sits untouched until its texture and flavor decline. Regular meals also reveal gaps in water, fuel, can openers, and recipes before an emergency.

For a dependable rule of thumb, rotate home-canned food within one year, commercial high-acid cans within 12 to 18 months, and commercial low-acid cans within 2 to 5 years. Once opened, refrigerate leftovers and use the shorter refrigerated timeline in the table. Safe processing and sound containers remain the foundation for every shelf-life estimate.
